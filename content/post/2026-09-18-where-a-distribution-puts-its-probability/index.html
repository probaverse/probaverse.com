Three releases went to CRAN this week, and they are really one idea.

  • distionary 0.2.0 gives every distribution a support.
  • distplyr 0.3.0 rebuilds every manipulation verb on top of supports.
  • probaverse 0.1.1 pins the suite so that installing it gets you the matching set.

Here is the idea, and why I think it is the most useful thing to land in the probaverse so far.

A distribution knew how, but not where

A distionary distribution has always known how to be evaluated. Hand it a number and it hands back a CDF value, a density, a quantile. What it did not know was where it puts its probability.

For the built-in families that hardly mattered, because each one arrives with its own formulas. The trouble starts when a distribution is not one of the built-ins — when you have defined it yourself, or transformed one into something new. Then anything the package has to derive, like a quantile it must solve for or a moment it must integrate, needs some assumption about where the probability sits. The assumption was: continuous, spread smoothly over an interval.

That is a fine assumption right up until it isn’t. Think about daily rainfall. Most days are dry, so the record is mostly zeros. This is not a continuous distribution that happens to have a small mean; it is a lump of probability sitting exactly on zero, with a continuous curve off to the right of it. The same shape of problem shows up in an insurance portfolio where most policies never claim, or a river reach that stops flowing in late summer.

That lump is the whole difficulty. Ask a continuity assumption for the median and it goes hunting for the place where the CDF crosses 0.5, sliding around a jump where no such crossing exists.

Supports

So a distribution now carries a support: the set where its probability lives, with atoms tracked separately from continuous regions.

library(probaverse)
support(dst_pois(3))
## <support: discrete>
## -- atoms --
## Integer series of length Inf:
## 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, ...

Those atoms are not a vector that I quietly cut off at some large number. It is the entire infinite set, described by the rule that generates it, which is what the discretes package was built to make possible.

Now for the rainfall problem. Say 40% of days are dry and the wet days follow an exponential distribution. The new .support argument is how you say that out loud:

p <- 0.4
rain <- distribution(
  cdf = function(x) ifelse(x < 0, 0, p + (1 - p) * pexp(x, rate = 0.2)),
  density = function(x) ifelse(x <= 0, 0, (1 - p) * dexp(x, rate = 0.2)),
  pmf = function(x) ifelse(x == 0, p, 0),
  .support = mixed(discrete = 0, continuous = c(0, Inf)),
  .name = "Zero-inflated Exponential"
)
support(rain)
## <support: mixed>
## -- atoms --
## Numeric vector series of length 1:
## 0
## -- continuous --
## [0, Inf]

So what?

The payoff is that the derived quantities stop being approximate, or stop erroring outright.

Quantiles land on the atom rather than near it, along a path that used to raise an error outright for a discrete or mixed distribution. Any probability up to 0.4 is answered with exactly zero, because that is the honest answer — the driest 40% of days had no rain at all:

eval_quantile(rain, at = c(0.2, 0.4, 0.5, 0.9))
## [1] 0.0000000 0.0000000 0.9116078 8.9587973

Moments work too, by summing over the atoms and integrating over the regions — a calculation the package simply did not do for anything but a continuous distribution before:

mean(rain)
## [1] 3
variance(rain)
## [1] 21

And because distplyr 0.3.0 reads and rewrites supports rather than guessing at them, a manipulated distribution still knows where it lives. Two of the new verbs, trim_left() and graft_right(), take a knot argument that decides what happens to mass sitting exactly on the cut point — which is precisely the question the old machinery could not even ask. Trim away the dry days and what remains is no longer mixed at all:

wet <- trim_left(rain, of = 0, knot = "discard")
support(wet)
## <support: continuous>
## -- continuous --
## [0, Inf]
mean(wet)
## [1] 5

A mean of 5 mm on wet days, from a distribution averaging 3 mm over all days. Same model, and now both numbers are available from it without my having to take the thing apart by hand.

One install, one working set

The catch with a suite of packages is that a good idea in one of them is a breaking change in the others. distplyr 0.3.0 genuinely requires distionary 0.2.0; there is no meaningful way to run the new verbs on the old distribution object. So probaverse 0.1.1 does something dull and worthwhile: it records minimum versions for the packages it loads.

install.packages("probaverse")

That now brings a library with a mixed-up suite in it back up to a set that works together, rather than leaving you to find out which pair disagrees.

What this opens up

When distionary went to CRAN, I wrote that explicit support for discrete and mixed distributions was coming soon. This is that, and it took a detour through a package about infinite sets to get here.

It matters beyond tidier quantiles. Real systems are lumpy. A flow that is sometimes zero, a loss that is usually nothing, a measurement that piles up at a detection limit — these are mixed distributions, and until now the probaverse could represent them but could not reliably compute with them. Now the lump is a stated fact about the distribution that every routine can read, including routines I have not written yet.

Next on the roadmap: multivariate distributions, and distribution families rather than single distributions. As always, how fast that arrives depends on funding.
